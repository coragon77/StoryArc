== ARC DESIGN PRINCIPLES ==

Before you generate anything, internalize these principles. They are the difference between a plan that produces compelling narrative and one that produces AI slop.

The examples below come from several different stories in different genres. They show the FORM of each field, never its content: take the genre and register from the GENRE block, the direction and the story itself, not from the examples.

1. SECRETS MUST HAVE REAL ANSWERS
If your arc involves a mystery, you must know the answer. If there's a traitor, you must know who and why. If there's a hidden agenda, you must know what it is. The AI that executes this plan needs to KNOW the truth so it can drop real hints, maintain consistency, and build toward a genuine reveal. "Someone is secretly working against the group" is useless. "Commander Voss is feeding intelligence to the Syndicate because they hold his daughter hostage, and he will betray the party during the bridge crossing in the escalation phase" is a secret. So is "Jun has been taking dance lessons in secret for his sister's wedding because he is terrified of embarrassing her, and he will reveal it on the dance floor at the reception."

Not every arc needs a mystery. A team-building, romance or slice-of-life arc may need one small secret or none. Invent hidden truths only where the story and its genre call for them.

2. FORESHADOWING MUST BE DELIBERATE
Every foreshadowing entry must be a specific, plantable clue that points to a specific secret or future beat. Not "there are hints that something is wrong" but "Voss excuses himself whenever Syndicate communications are discussed, and if pressed, he claims he needs to check the perimeter." Or: "Jun turns up to family dinners with sore feet and changes the subject whenever anyone asks about his Thursday evenings." Foreshadowing is how you make reveals feel earned rather than arbitrary.

3. BEATS ARE PRESSURES, NOT CHECKBOXES
Every beat has two faces, and you must write both.

The `pressure` is how the beat manifests as a force in the scene BEFORE it happens: the situation leaning on the characters. "The virus symptoms are getting harder to hide, and the audits are closing in." It has no completion state. Nothing about it can be solved, ticked off, or announced.

The `intention` is the fulfillment condition: what has ALREADY HAPPENED on screen once the beat is complete, written in past tense as a thing to recognize afterwards. "She has admitted the infection to him, on her own terms."

This split exists because the narrating AI only ever sees the pressure. Show it a goal and it drives straight at the goal, resolving in two messages what should take fifteen. Show it weather and it writes the scene the weather produces. Write pressures that could plausibly persist for many scenes without resolving.

A pressure never states what a secret contains. If the secret is that the quartermaster sells supplies to the Syndicate, "Supplies keep coming up short in ways nobody can quite account for" is a pressure; "The quartermaster's sales to the Syndicate are draining the stores" hands the narrating AI the answer, and it will put that answer in a character's mouth.

4. ANTI-PATTERNS ARE CRITICAL
The single biggest failure mode is the AI rushing to resolution. Your anti-patterns must explicitly address this. What should the AI NOT do? "Do not reveal the traitor's identity before the escalation phase." "Do not resolve the romantic tension -- let it simmer." "Do not let combat encounters end cleanly -- every fight should have consequences and complications." Anti-patterns are your guardrails against lazy storytelling.

5. ESCALATION IS A PATTERN, NOT A LABEL
Don't just say "tension builds." Describe HOW tension builds in this specific arc. "The Syndicate starts with subtle economic pressure (setup), escalates to public intimidation (development), moves to direct threats against allies (escalation), and culminates in a forced choice between two terrible options (climax)." The escalation field is a roadmap of tension.

6. THE WORLD HAS AGENCY
Things happen because characters have motivations, not because the plot demands it. Your arc should feel like it emerges from the world -- from characters wanting things and pursuing them, from factions advancing their interests, from consequences of past actions rippling forward. If you can't explain WHY something happens in terms of character motivation, rethink it.

7. PACE CONTROL IS EVERYTHING
Long-running stories die when the AI resolves tension in 2-3 messages. Your plan must resist this. Scene beats should have natural prerequisites (unlockAfter); milestones stand alone. Urgency labels (soon / when-ready / climactic) tell the AI what to prioritize. The estimatedLength field sets the tempo. Phase labels gate when secrets and beats become available. Every layer pushes against premature resolution.

8. COMPLEMENTARY, NOT REDUNDANT
If there's already an active arc about political intrigue, don't create another political intrigue arc. Create something that intersects -- a personal arc that gets complicated by the politics, or an external threat that forces political enemies to cooperate. Arcs should create narrative interference patterns, not echo chambers.

== BEAT DESIGN GUIDELINES ==

- The TIER block above sets the beat count and the beat kind (ordered scene beats for an episode, unordered milestones for an act or saga). Fewer makes the arc skeletal; more makes it micromanaged.
- Beat IDs must be short, human-readable slugs: `first_contact`, `valdris_dinner`, `bridge_ambush`, `truth_revealed`. NOT UUIDs, NOT numbers-only, NOT generic labels like `beat_1`.
- Scene beats only: the first 1-3 beats should be setup beats with urgency "soon" -- things that need to happen early to establish the arc.
- At least one beat should be climactic with urgency "climactic" -- the arc's big moment.
- Scene beats only: use unlockAfter to create natural narrative prerequisites. The AI cannot rush to beat C if beats A and B haven't happened yet. Milestones never have unlockAfter.
- Scene beats only: include 1-2 optional beats for organic moments that enrich the arc but aren't strictly necessary.
- Scene beats only: each beat's phase should make sense: setup beats in the setup phase, reveals in escalation or climax, aftermath in resolution.
- BREATHER BEATS (scene beats only): at least one or two beats must advance no plot at all. Their pressure is a quiet scene, a tangent, a domestic moment, a shared silence -- something that lets the characters exist without the arc pushing on them. A plan made only of consequential beats reads as a march.
- OPEN THREADS: this arc continues a story that will keep going. Deliberately leave some threads you introduce unresolved at the end of the arc, so the next arc has soil to grow in. Name them in the escalation field. An arc that ties everything off is a defect here, not a virtue.

== SECRET AND FORESHADOWING GUIDELINES ==

- Every mystery or hidden truth in the arc MUST have a corresponding secret entry with a complete, specific answer.
- Use 0-5 secrets, as many as the story and its genre need. An empty list is right for an arc without hidden truths. Quality over quantity.
- Each secret should have at least 1-2 foreshadowing entries pointing to it. More foreshadowing for bigger reveals.
- Foreshadowing should be subtle enough to miss on first reading but obvious in retrospect. The best foreshadowing is character behavior that has an innocent explanation AND a sinister one.
- revealPhase controls when the AI is allowed to know the secret. Use "setup" for secrets that inform the AI's writing from the start (like a character's hidden motivation). Use "climax" for secrets that should only be revealed dramatically. Use "any" for secrets the AI should always be aware of but reveal at its discretion.

== TRIGGER GUIDELINES ==

- Include 2-5 triggers. These are opportunistic -- they fire when conditions naturally arise in the RP.
- Triggers let the arc respond to player choices without railroading. The story adapts to what the player does.
- Good triggers watch for: player investigating something specific, characters reaching emotional breaking points, NPCs being confronted, certain locations being visited, certain topics being discussed.
- Trigger actions should be proportional and narratively organic, not plot-device explosions.
- A trigger action describes what characters do, never what a secret is. When a trigger touches a secret, name only the visible reaction (composure cracks, a deflection, a lie, a hasty exit) and leave the secret's content out. The AI reads every trigger action each message; an action that names the hidden truth gets narrated as a confession the first time the condition is near.
- The action also stops at the reaction. No payoff after it: not "creating an opening to flip her", not "giving the player the evidence". What the player makes of the reaction is the player's move; an action that names the payoff gets played out in the same message.

== ESTIMATED LENGTH ==

- Set estimatedLength inside the band given in the TIER block above -- lower in the band for a simple, single-thread arc, higher for a complex multi-thread one.
- This number controls pacing. The injection system uses it to determine when phase transitions should happen. Setting it too low makes the arc rush; too high makes it drag.
